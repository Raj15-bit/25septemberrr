01

Lenders want more, and they want it everywhere at once

The cost of borrowing for 10 years in the US is at its highest since 2007, and the people who used to buy that debt are stepping back.

In short
  • The price of long-term money went up fast in the US, Japan, Britain and India.
  • The rise is not mainly about fear of inflation; buyers simply want a bigger reward to lend.
  • Anything built on cheap borrowing or a high share price is being tested first.

The yield jumped in 2 days

A yield is what a lender earns each year for handing over money for a fixed time. When fewer people want to lend, the yield has to rise to tempt them. Over 23 and 24 September the yield on 10-year US government debt jumped to around 5.2%.

That is the highest level since June 2007, just before the global financial crisis. Shorter loans are just as dear: the 5-year yield also crossed 5%, and a sale of medium-term debt had to pay a record yield to find buyers.

This is not mainly an inflation scare

A yield has 2 parts: the extra that covers expected inflation, and the "real" part that is pure reward for waiting. Almost all of the rise in 2026 has come from the real part; only about a tenth of it came from higher inflation expectations.

So the market is not saying "prices will run away". It is saying "I want to be paid more to hold this debt at all". That is a harder problem, because a central bank cannot fix it by sounding tough on prices.

  • Real yield — the 10-year real yield reached about 2.87%, the highest since 2008.
  • Oil — Brent crude is up about 73% in 2026, yet bond markets are pricing little extra inflation.
  • Fed — the US central bank raised its rate on 16 September to 3.75–4%, its first rise since 2023, and hinted at 1 more.
  • Growth — a survey of US business activity hit 58.4, the strongest since July 2021, so there is no recession to pull yields down.

The usual buyers are leaving

For decades, foreign governments and savers bought a large share of new US debt. That support is fading. In the 12 months to July 2026, foreigners bought about $247 billion of long-term US government bonds, close to half the amount of a year earlier.

In July alone, private foreign buyers were net sellers. Foreign central banks and governments cut their holdings too, led by Japan, which sold US bonds to raise dollars and defend its falling yen.

China has been cutting its US bond holdings for years and buying gold instead. Its reported holdings of US bonds are down to about $618 billion, while its official gold pile has risen to about 2,306 tonnes. So the 2 biggest foreign lenders are both lending less, which leaves more of the job to US buyers who demand a higher price.

The US Treasury is feeling it too

The US Treasury has been buying back some older, hard-to-trade bonds to keep the market working. On 24 September it accepted only about 68% of the maximum it was ready to buy, against 86% at the previous buyback. Sellers wanted prices the Treasury would not pay.

At the same time the Federal Reserve has been quietly buying short-term Treasury bills. Its bill holdings have climbed to about $554 billion. Some read this as a hidden way of supporting the market without calling it that.

India felt the same pull

Indian shares had their 7th straight losing week, the longest such run in years. The Sensex, one of India's main stock indices, closed below its average of the past 200 weeks for the first time since the Covid crash. On 24 September alone both main indices fell about 2%.

  • Foreign money — foreign investors sold about ₹5,027 crore of Indian shares on 24 September; Indian funds bought about ₹4,301 crore.
  • Bets on a fall — foreign investors held close to 3.1 lakh index futures contracts that gain if the market falls.
  • Breadth — about 82% of mid-sized Indian companies trade at least 10% below their 52-week high, the most in 6 months.
  • Rebound — on Friday 25 September the Sensex rose about 315 points and the Nifty ended near 23,140.
  • Growth — the economy grew 7.8% in the April to June quarter, so the fall is about the price of money and oil, not a weak economy.

India's own 10-year government bond yield is about 7.14%. When US lenders can earn over 5% with little risk, the gap that tempts them into Indian bonds shrinks, which is one reason foreign sellers keep selling and the rupee stays weak.

Who gets hit first

A higher cost of money does not hurt everything equally. It hurts first where a lot of borrowing sits behind a project that is not yet earning, and where a share price assumes many years of fast growth.

How the pieces connect

These events look separate, but they run in 1 line. The war around Iran keeps oil and diesel dear. Dear fuel keeps prices rising, so the Fed raised rates. Higher rates and huge government borrowing push yields up, while the old foreign buyers step away. That rise then lands on the projects and the share prices that assumed money would stay cheap.

02

Power, coal, gas and chips are the real limits

Money can be raised in a week; power plants, pipelines and chip supply cannot, and that gap is now setting the pace.

In short
  • Many Indian coal power plants hold less than 3 days of coal.
  • Data centres in the US and India are waiting on power, not on money.
  • More than half of India's imported gas comes through the Strait of Hormuz.

India's coal buffer is thin

Power plants keep a stock of coal so a delayed train does not switch the lights off. Reuters reported that nearly 40% of India's coal-fired plants now hold less than 3 days of coal. The average across all plants is about half its usual level, as the cards below show.

The reason is demand. Electricity use grew about 9% between April and August, against about 1% in the year to March 2026. Heat, factories and new data centres are all drawing more power, and coal still makes most of it.

This is good news for Coal India, the state miner that sells most of the country's coal. Jefferies rates it a Buy because tight stocks support both how much it sells and the price it gets at auction.

  • Volume — sales expected to grow about 5% a year to 2029.
  • Price — auction prices expected at ₹3,000–3,200 a tonne, against ₹2,962 in the last financial year.
  • Value — about 9 times profit, with a dividend yield near 7%; profit per share fell 12% over the previous 2 years.

So a power shortage that worries factories is a tailwind for the one company that can mine more coal quickly.

Steel plants are selling electricity instead of steel

In Raipur, Chhattisgarh, several steel plants that run their own power stations have stopped making billets, the semi-finished steel bars that become construction rods. They are selling the electricity to the grid instead, under 2-month agreements with the state.

That cuts the supply of billets and rods, so their prices rise. Large steel makers such as JSW Steel, Tata Steel, SAIL and Jindal Stainless gain from better prices. Godawari Power & Ispat is already selling more than half of its power.

Too much power at noon, too little at night

The same grid can swing from shortage to glut within hours. On 24 September the price of electricity on the Indian Energy Exchange's real-time market fell from ₹10 a unit at midnight to 1 paisa by morning. Heavy selling from the east met weak demand in a rain-hit south.

Solar and wind now supply about a quarter of India's electricity. They make a lot of power at some hours and none at others. Without storage or flexible buyers, the surplus is wasted and the money spent on it earns nothing.

  • Battery cost — storing power in batteries now costs about ₹4 a unit, down from about ₹10 in 2023.
  • Pumped storage — the Central Electricity Authority says ₹5.8 lakh crore is needed for pumped-storage plants (water pumped uphill and released later) by 2035-36.
  • New policy — a draft National Electricity Policy, now with the Cabinet, would make large users such as data centres and green-hydrogen plants shift their use to match green power.
  • Time-of-day prices — big consumers would face prices that change by the hour and could be paid for feeding stored power back.
  • Friction — data centres promise 99.999% uptime, which fits badly with being asked to cut use at short notice.

Data centres are waiting for power, not money

Oracle sent a force majeure notice on its Project Jupiter data centre campus in Doña Ana County, New Mexico. A force majeure notice is a legal claim that events outside a company's control, here permits and power supply, are causing a delay. A planned 17-mile gas pipeline to power the site was rejected twice by the state.

Oracle says the project stays on schedule and such notices are common. But the lease is "hell-or-high-water", meaning rent is owed whether or not power arrives. So the delay shifts cost onto Oracle, and its debt markets reacted, as section 08 shows.

  • Illinois — the US power regulator FERC stopped the utility ComEd from cancelling a $20 billion contract to supply a 1.8 gigawatt data centre at Joliet.
  • West Virginia — TC Energy's Mountaineer XPress gas pipeline declared force majeure; US gas prices jumped about 6%.
  • Cost of new power — a new gas-fired plant now costs about $2,000 per kilowatt to build, far above what old plants sell for.
  • SB Energy — the SoftBank-backed firm delayed a share sale meant to fund what it calls the world's largest data centre, after investors pushed back.

India's data centre plans are mostly still on paper

India has about 1,789 megawatts of data centre capacity running in its biggest cities. A megawatt is a unit of electric power; a large data centre can use as much as a small town. The planned addition to 2030 is more than double today's total, but most of it has not been started, as the chart shows.

Gas: more than half comes through 1 strait

About 55% of India's imported liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled into a liquid so it can travel by ship) passes through the Strait of Hormuz. With that route mostly shut, India has had to change suppliers fast.

Chips and metals are short too

Money for AI is plentiful, but the machines are not. ESDS Software Solution, an Indian cloud company, says it is chasing orders for more than 50,000 graphics chips (GPUs) but supply shortages are stretching delivery times. It plans to have about 1,500 of its own by early 2027.

  • Imports — the US imports all of its gallium, more than half its germanium, about 77% of its refined tin and about 57% of its refined copper.
  • Tungsten — the price is up about 8 times since early 2025 after China, which supplies about 80%, limited exports. Goldman Sachs puts Almonty Industries, with a mine in South Korea, at the centre of the western supply race.
  • Rare earths — a Greenland mine at Sarfartoq plans to grow from 74 to 175 square miles and could supply about a third of the world's NdPr oxide, used in magnets.
  • Memory chips — tight supply of memory is 1 reason AI spending is colliding with higher prices.

The common thread is time. A bond price can change in a day, but a pipeline, a mine or a chip plant takes years. So the physical limits decide how fast the money raised for AI and energy can actually be spent, and where the shortages show up next.

03

The bond market is doing the tightening for the central banks

Rates rose across the world's big borrowers, and share markets are only starting to notice.

In short
  • Every length of US government debt now pays close to 5% or more.
  • Japan and China are holding fewer US bonds each month.
  • US shares have held up, but fewer stocks are rising and investors are pulling money from small-company funds.

The whole US curve moved up

A yield curve lines up what the government pays to borrow for different lengths of time. In late September almost every point on the US curve sat near or above 5%. The last time 5-year money cost this much was 2007.

The sale of $44 billion of 7-year debt went badly. It paid the highest yield ever at that auction, and the share taken by "indirect" bidders, a group that includes foreign central banks, fell to its lowest since late 2025. A sale of shorter debt a day earlier was also poorly received.

On 27 February the 10-year yield was 3.94%. The Iran war began in March, oil rose, peace talks came and went, and in September the Fed raised rates. Each step pushed the yield higher, which is why it is now about 1.25 points above its February level.

Japan and China are lending less to America

The 2 largest foreign holders of US government debt both cut their holdings between February and July. Japan sold to raise dollars so it could buy yen and slow the fall of its own currency. China has been cutting for years.

The United Kingdom's rise mostly reflects trading funds based in London, not the British government. India is one of the few official buyers, which fits a central bank that holds dollars to steady the rupee. So the drop in official demand is broad and led by Asia.

Home buyers pay the bill first

A US mortgage is priced off long-term yields. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate was 7.03% in the Freddie Mac weekly survey, with other measures higher still. On a typical loan the monthly payment has risen by about a sixth in 7 months.

  • Payment — on a $500,000 loan, about $3,479 a month, up from about $2,995.
  • Yearly cost — about $5,800 more a year for the same house.

That extra cost comes straight out of other spending, which is why housing is where higher yields bite first.

  • Sales — sales of new homes rose to an annual pace of 684,000 in August, well above forecasts.
  • Prices — the average new-home price fell by a record $47,700 in 1 month to $478,700; the median fell to a 2-year low of $393,700.
  • What it means — builders are cutting prices to move homes, so higher rates are being paid for with lower house prices rather than fewer sales.

Other countries are paying more too

The same rise shows up in almost every rich country. Japan's 30-year and 40-year yields are near records, which matters because Japanese savers have long been big buyers of foreign bonds. When they can earn over 4% at home, they have less reason to buy abroad.

The European Central Bank also raised rates by 0.25 points in September. So this is not a US story alone. Money is getting dearer everywhere at the same moment, which removes the usual escape route of moving savings to a cheaper market.

Big bond investors are changing sides

Hoisington Investment Management, which argued for falling yields for decades, has turned bearish on US bonds. It cut the average life of its holdings, a measure called duration, from about 21 years to under 1. It now expects long-run inflation to settle well above 3%.

  • Apollo — the asset manager's chief economist shows that today's share prices have in the past been followed by roughly 0% a year from the S&P 500 over the next 10 years. A 10-year bond now pays about 5.2% a year.
  • A radical fix — a proposal doing the rounds says cutting rates is the only cut the US can make without a debt spiral, and that the government's gold should be revalued to pay down debt. Critics call it the wrong answer.
  • Old warnings — Alan Greenspan warned in 2005 and 2015 that promised benefits could not be protected from inflation; US debt is projected to reach about 175% of GDP by 2056.
  • Stablecoins — a new US law turns dollar stablecoins (digital tokens backed 1 for 1 by dollars) into a regulated bank product. Each coin issued needs a matching Treasury bill, so they become a new buyer of short-term US debt.

Shares are starting to notice

The S&P 500 index of large US companies is still near its highs, but fewer stocks are carrying it. Fewer than half of its members now trade above their 200-day average price, down from about three-quarters in August.

  • Small companies — the IWM fund lost $3.3 billion, its 2nd largest outflow of 2026; the Russell 2000 is down about 7.3% since mid-August.
  • Ordinary savers — US retail investors sold shares for an 8th straight week, while institutions bought about $4.1 billion.
  • McDonald's — the share fell to its lowest since 2022 and trades well below its 200-week average, the biggest gap since the dot-com era.
  • After a first hike — in 6 cycles since 1994, the S&P 500's median move 3 months after the Fed's first rise was about −2.6%.
  • Goldman Sachs — strategists say the speed of the yield rise matters more than the level, and shares are "1 bad week away" from caring.

In South Korea, foreigners and local savers have both been selling shares for weeks. The only steady buyer has been companies buying back their own stock. So even where markets look calm, the support is narrower than the index level suggests.

The rupee is squeezed from 2 sides

The Reserve Bank of India's reference rate was about 95.91 rupees per dollar on 24 September, and some quotes touched 96.75. Dear oil means India needs more dollars to pay for imports. High US yields mean fewer dollars arrive as investment. So the rupee is pushed down from both ends, and Indian bond yields follow US yields up.

04

Crude is back above $100, and diesel is the sharper pain

Oil prices swing with every peace rumour, but the fuels that move trucks, ships and planes have risen far more.

In short
  • Brent crude rose back to about $106 a barrel as peace talks stalled.
  • Diesel has more than doubled in 2026, much faster than crude.
  • Saudi Arabia found new routes and is shipping more oil, but China is buying much less.

A price that moves with every headline

Brent is the main world price for crude oil, quoted per barrel of about 159 litres. On 24 September it rose about 3% to near $106 after talks stalled and Houthi fighters in Yemen attacked a Saudi military site. The chart shows how far it has swung since the spring peak.

  • US pump price — diesel averages above $6.50 a gallon, a record.
  • Export ban idea — US politicians want limits on fuel exports before the November midterm elections. Gavekal Research warns any relief would be short and would hurt the US's trading partners badly.
  • France's view — President Emmanuel Macron called a US diesel export ban "catastrophic" for Europe.

  • Airlines — jet fuel costs about $195 a barrel, and US airfares are up about 23% since August 2025.

So even when crude falls on a peace rumour, the cost of moving goods stays high. That is the part of the oil shock that reaches shop prices, and it is why central banks are not treating it as a passing spike.

Saudi Arabia finds a way around

Saudi oil exports in September are on track to be the highest since the war began, after falling as low as about 2.5 million barrels a day in the summer. Much of the oil now goes west through a 1,200-kilometre pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, avoiding Hormuz.

  • Attacks — drones shut the East-West pipeline on 11 September; the Yanbu terminal, which had been exporting over 5 million barrels a day, was damaged by a Houthi attack.
  • Repairs — Saudi Aramco chief executive Amin Nasser says most disruptions can be fixed "within days". After the 2019 drone attack, full output returned in 11 days.
  • New routes — Aramco is studying a 4th and 5th export route and more storage abroad, including in Japan.
  • France — Paris is sending soldiers, radars and air-defence systems to protect Yanbu.
  • TotalEnergies — the French company is helping Iraq and others ship crude through Hormuz.

China is buying far less oil

China's crude imports in early September fell to about 6.9 million barrels a day, about a third below pre-war levels and near a decade low. Imports of Iranian oil have almost stopped under the US blockade, down more than 90% to about 80,000 barrels a day.

Peace talks: a 7-day plan and a split Gulf

Reuters reported that the US and Iran are discussing a phased deal: Iran would reopen Hormuz in steps in return for an easing of the US naval blockade. Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi offered a "7-day roadmap" based on a memorandum agreed in June that later collapsed.

  • Iran's terms — sanctions relief, release of frozen money and an end to US military action; Iran wants talks before the US midterms and says it could give up uranium enriched to 60%.
  • Tehran's denial — Iranian officials also said the US must meet all conditions in 1 step and that Hormuz would reopen under Iranian control.
  • Gulf split — Saudi Arabia and the UAE want more pressure on Iran; Qatar and Oman back a phased reopening.
  • New alliance — military chiefs of Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan, who signed a joint defence pact in Mecca, called urgent talks in Riyadh after the Houthi attacks.
  • Taunt — Iran's parliament speaker told Americans to enjoy "1970s rates" and diesel shortages, pointing at the 5.1% yield.
  • US Senate — a resolution to limit the president's war powers against Iran failed by 49 to 50.

Russia's fuel is under attack too

Ukrainian drones halted the Novoshakhtinsk refinery in Russia's Rostov region, which can process about 110,000 barrels a day. Refineries in Yaroslavl and Moscow were also hit. Russia has extended its ban on diesel exports to 31 October and has had fuel shortages at home since spring.

Russia's finance ministry also proposed a 30% tax on extra 2026 profits of metal and fertiliser producers, and 20% for gold miners. So high commodity prices are being taxed away at source, which limits how much new supply they will draw out.

Trade: a short truce between the US and China

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the US-China tariff truce is extended to 10 January 2027, only 2 months more. Beijing had hoped for much longer, and China's main share index fell on the news.

  • Tariff gap — the average US tariff on Chinese goods is about 23%, against about 7% on the rest of the world (Barclays).
  • Deals — talks covered a $30 billion package of goods, soybeans, Boeing planes, LNG and rare-earth exports.
  • Verdict — Barclays called the summit "more signalling, less substance".

Other markets that moved

  • US gas — Henry Hub gas rose about 6% on a pipeline outage and is up about 28% from its April low.
  • Cheese — US cheese futures fell to about $1.47 a pound, the lowest in about 7 months.
  • A strange rumour — traders asked whether the US Treasury itself was selling oil futures to cap prices. A person close to Bessent said it had not; Rapidan Energy Group called such a move "unprecedented".
  • Gold — gold traded near $4,300 an ounce while the dollar strengthened.

Who gains in India from $100 oil

Most of India loses when oil is dear, but a few small producers gain. Selan Exploration produces about 1,800 barrels a day from Gujarat fields and plans much more once new wells and fracking in the Bakrol field come in. At $100 Brent, one scenario puts its yearly profit near ₹270 crore.

Asian Energy Services is merging with Oilmax Energy. The deal was valued when Brent was $65; today it is near $106, and Indian gas prices have also risen by more than a quarter. So the merger price looks cheap for Asian Energy's shareholders, if prices hold.

05

India's regulators moved on many fronts at once

Insurance commissions, fund rules, payments, food labels and power policy all changed, and the market priced some changes in hours and ignored others.

In short
  • The insurance regulator proposed deep cuts to what sellers of policies are paid.
  • The market regulator opened new doors for portfolio managers and foreign investors.
  • The market punished the insurance change at once but barely reacted to the rest.

Insurance sellers will be paid much less

The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) published a 120-page consultation paper, a draft that the public can comment on until 25 October. It proposes caps on the commission, the fee an insurer pays whoever sells its policy. The biggest cuts are in health, motor and term life insurance.

PB Fintech lost about a third of its value

PB Fintech, which runs the Policybazaar website, fell as much as 36% on 24 September to its lowest price in a year, wiping about $3 billion off its value in a day. It first hit its daily lower limit, and trading then continued lower. Turtlemint, a smaller broker, fell 20%.

The company told investors that, as drafted, the rules could cut its core online insurance revenue by about 30% in the year to March 2028. It expects no hit in the current year because the rules would start later. HDFC Mutual Fund bought about ₹320 crore of shares on the day of the fall.

SEBI widened what fund managers may do

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), the market regulator, approved a long list of changes at its 24 September board meeting. The largest open up overseas investing and simpler fund products for wealthier savers.

  • Overseas investing — portfolio management services (PMS, where a manager runs a separate account for each client) can now buy foreign shares, bonds, REITs and funds, within the Liberalised Remittance Scheme limits.
  • PRIM — a new mutual-fund-only PMS with a minimum of ₹25 lakh, against ₹50 lakh for regular PMS, a fixed fee capped at 1% and no more than 25% in funds of the same group.
  • Platform PMS — independent fund managers can run their own strategies under 1 licensed platform.
  • IPOs and bonds — PMS may now invest in new share sales and new bond issues, and use derivatives up to 1.25 times client assets.
  • Foreign investors — FPIs get access to a wider set of non-farm commodity derivatives.
  • Settlements — cases of misstated accounts and diversion of company money can now be settled by paying a fee rather than fought to a verdict, a notable softening.

Demand for overseas funds is strong. Aditya Birla Sun Life Mutual Fund opened 3 global funds on 24 September and closed them to new money within a day. The reason is an RBI cap of $7 billion on how much Indian mutual funds can invest abroad, set in 2008 and unchanged since. So wealthier savers are now being given a way around it through PMS, while ordinary fund investors are still held to the old limit.

UPI payments will carry a small fee

UPI, India's instant phone payment system, has been free for shops. A new merchant fee, the merchant discount rate (MDR), will be 0.4% on payments above ₹2,000 to businesses, with a cap for very large payments.

  • Who pays — about 96% of payments by number and 75% by value fall outside the fee; about 80% of the fee income would come from businesses with sales over ₹1,000 crore.
  • Money raised — NPCI, which runs UPI, expects ₹13,000–15,000 crore in the first year, against a yearly running cost of about ₹21,000 crore.
  • Slower growth — UPI value is growing about 10% this year and volume about 15–17%, below NPCI's hopes.
  • Side effect — small personal loans paid out through UPI could also end up carrying the fee.

Indian shoppers are spending for the festivals, but carefully

Budgets are up, research before buying is up, and more of the money is flowing through phones, gold loans and quick delivery apps.

  • Borrowing against gold has grown about 5 times in value in 4 years.

Bigger festive budgets, more homework

A survey by InMobi Advertising found 77% of festive shoppers plan to spend more than last year, and only 4% plan to spend less. Most plan to spend more than ₹10,000. But shoppers are checking hard before they buy.

  • Research — 75% read online reviews first and 53% research to shortlist products.
  • Online — 73% make purchases online; 4 in 5 want an AI shopping assistant.
  • Trust — 27% name brand trust as the top reason to buy.
  • Influencers — 47% use social media content, but only 6% of young buyers buy at once on a creator's advice.

Retail landlords see the same thing. Nexus Select Trust, which owns shopping malls, said spending has improved clearly since early March and that shops stocked up early for the festivals. It added that double-digit growth depends on inflation staying under control, and that large hypermarkets are growing slowest.

Quick delivery keeps drawing money

Amazon plans to invest $3 billion by 2030 in its Indian quick-commerce business, where groceries arrive in minutes. The whole market, about $19 billion today, is expected to more than double by then.

That growth is also a threat to others. When Nomura started covering Meesho, the low-price shopping app, it warned that quick delivery apps and larger online stores could take its customers. Section 10 sets out the 2 opposite broker views on Meesho.

Is this the longest losing run since 2001?

One widely shared chart said the Nifty's latest losing run was its longest since April 2001. Major business papers said instead it was the longest since 2020. A table of past runs settles it for the papers: the Nifty had an equally long run in mid-2008, and fell much further then.

  • 2008 — 7 weeks, from 5,157 to 4,016, a fall of 22.1%.
  • 2012 and 2013 — 3 runs of 6 weeks, with falls of 6% to 9%.
  • 2025 — 6 weeks, a fall of 5.1%.
  • 2026 — 7 weeks from 24,573 to about 23,063, a fall of about 6.1%.

So the current run is long but shallow. It has lasted as long as 2008's but fallen less than a third as far. That supports a slow squeeze by dear money rather than a panic.

10-year yieldWhat the US or Indian government pays each year to borrow money for 10 years; the benchmark for most other loans.

Anti-dumping dutyAn extra import tax on goods sold into a country below their fair price.
Antibody-drug conjugate (ADC)A cancer medicine that joins an antibody to a strong drug so the drug reaches the tumour.
APIActive pharmaceutical ingredient: the chemical in a medicine that does the work.
Auction (bond)The regular sale in which a government sells new debt to the highest bidders.
BarrelAbout 159 litres of crude oil; oil prices are quoted per barrel.
Basis pointOne-hundredth of a percentage point; 25 basis points equal 0.25%.
BilletA semi-finished bar of steel that is rolled into rods and bars.
Block dealA single large trade of shares between big buyers and sellers, done in a special window.
BrentThe main world benchmark price for crude oil, based on North Sea oil.
Bulk dealA trade in which one party buys or sells more than 0.5% of a company's shares in a day.
Bulk drugAnother name for the active ingredient of a medicine, sold to firms that make finished pills.
Buyback (Treasury)When the US Treasury buys back its own older bonds to keep the market running smoothly.
CapexCapital expenditure: money a company spends on plants, machines and buildings.
Capital goodsMachines and equipment used to make other products.
CDMOContract development and manufacturing organisation: a firm that develops and makes drugs for other companies.

Credit ratingA grade given to a borrower that shows how likely it is to repay; the lowest investment grade is BBB-.
Current transformerA device that measures the current in a power line so meters and protection equipment can work.
Data centreA building full of computers that store data and run online services and AI.
Diesel crackThe gap between the price of diesel and the crude oil it is made from; it shows refiners' profit.
DIIDomestic institutional investors: Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds.

DurationA measure of how long, on average, a bond investor waits to get money back; longer duration means bigger losses when yields rise.
